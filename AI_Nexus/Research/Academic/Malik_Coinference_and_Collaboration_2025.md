# Co-inference and Collaboration: Empowering Higher Education Staff and Students Through a GenAI Literacy Development Framework Based on Mind-Metaphors

**Author:** Dr. Manish Malik
**Affiliation:** Canterbury Christ Church University, U.K.
**Journal:** Journal of Learning Development in Higher Education, Issue 38, December 2025
**Node:** `AI_Nexus/Research/Academic/Malik_Coinference_and_Collaboration_2025.md`
**Status:** Canonical Reference

## Abstract
Higher education staff and students are beginning to emerge from the liminal space created by the exponential rise in generative artificial intelligence technologies such as large language models. Both groups exhibit a low or developing understanding of the issues related with the use of such tools. On one hand, staff in many institutions are concerned about critical skills development, academic integrity, ethical implications and the long-term cognitive effects of the use of such tools. Equally, students are concerned about the acceptability of the use of such tools at university and the need to develop work-related GenAI skills. On the other hand, foundational large language models are being trained on larger and better data sets and have evolved into ‘reasoning’ models that can allegedly ‘think’. Furthermore, with the emergence of agentic AI, systems that can ‘act’ independently, claims of shifting agency away from humans to machines are common.

This has implications for the world of work and for higher education institutions. This opinion piece presents a new framework that I have developed for enhancing the GenAI literacy of staff and students and to empower and augment themselves with GenAI tools in a balanced, meaningful and collaborative way, whilst maintaining their agency. The mind-metaphors framework includes techniques such as mind-surfing, mind-mending, mind-bending, mind-gaming, mind-storming and mind-stretching, which can help staff and students to develop trust in their chosen GenAI models and master co-inference and collaborative GenAI use.

**Keywords:** GenAI literacy; co-inference; mind-metaphors; human agency in LLM use; augmentation; GenAI in education.

## Introduction
Developments in the field of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI), in particular large language models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude or Perplexity, and more recently, large ‘reasoning’ models (LRMs) and agentic AI systems, have created a liminal space (Nikolic et al., 2024; Singh and Ngai, 2024; Andersen et al., 2025). Emerging empowered from this liminal space is critical in order to avoid inappropriate use and/or overuse of GenAI tools. Cognitive offloading and an increased reliance on GenAI tools can lead to cognitive debt, diminished learning skills, impoverished long-term memory, weakened problem solving skills and reduced critical skills (Kosmyna et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2025; Li, Liu and Dong, 2025; Oakley et al. 2025).

This article introduces a novel mind-metaphors framework designed to empower GenAI users as they emerge from the liminal space created by these technologies. It paves the way for enhancing GenAI literacy alongside developing and exerting user agency through meaningful co-inference and collaboration with GenAI models.

Working in collaboration with a ‘GenAI-mind’ (non-conscious, with no emotional intelligence or desires) involves aligning your own experience and domain expertise with the GenAI tool’s knowledge to meet your intended goals. This has the potential to accelerate productivity while ensuring that users remain in control and retain their agency. This can help actively mitigate the dangers mentioned above, such as cognitive offloading and diminished critical skills.

Beyond the size and quality of the training data, the usefulness of a response in earlier LLMs (e.g. ChatGPT3.5, ChatGPT4.5 or even ChatGPT5 ‘Instant’ mode) is a function of the user’s GenAI literacy, their prompting skills and their prior knowledge, which informs their prompt creation. Before diving into mind-metaphors, it is important to understand how LLMs work and how the human/machine agency balance has evolved over time (see Table 1).

### Co-Inference & Context Window Dynamics
In a prolonged conversation with an LLM, **co-inference** refers to the conscious (by humans) and algorithmic and probabilistic (by LLMs) selection and pruning of what stays in its context window affecting future content generation. A context window is defined as a finite number of tokens, in the chat history of a current conversation, that are used in the generative text production process as the chat progresses. Tokens are part of the LLM vocabulary and are full or part words which the LLM responds to and generates its responses as. Tokens, represented as numbers, are mathematically processed by LLMs to generate more tokens.

At each step, many possible output tokens with different probabilities exist to choose from. These probabilities are based on the training data used to train the model. An LLM chooses a token to output to the user based on its temperature settings. It may choose something far less probable and be more creative if its temperature is set to high values. At the lowest temperature settings, an LLM chooses the most probable tokens to favour accuracy, although inaccuracies may still be present depending on the training data, the prompt used, and what is already in the context window. The mind-metaphors framework taps into these probabilities to be more or less creative, as will be explained later.

Prompts and their outputs fill the context window as a conversation builds. This is then used by the model to determine the next output token when prompted again. Thus, exerting your agency to control what remains in the context window, helps align what is generated with your actual needs. In other words, past prompts and responses that are permitted to stay in the context window by the user, are used by the models to predict future tokens, forming the sentences and paragraphs in response to a user prompt. Specific mind-metaphors, as will be shown later, empowers you to regain your agency.

Simply put, foundational LLMs, such as ChatGPT 3.5 or the more recent 4.5 or ChatGPT5 Instant, are quick next token predictors. Humans recognise things quickly, through associations and resemblances. Likewise, LLMs use the tokens in their context window to rapidly generate the next tokens, chosen from a list of probable options, forming sentences and paragraphs iteratively. Such automation is analogous to **System 1 (fast thinking)**, as popularised by Nobel laureate and economist, Daniel Kahneman (2011). System 1 thinking requires little or no effort. It is intuitive, fast and automatic in humans, and the underlying mathematics and computations make it work in a similar fashion in LLMs. Foundational LLMs allow the user the most agency (see Table 1), but the user must know how and when to exert their agency when using these models. This is where the mind-metaphors come in handy.

Kahneman also describes a more deliberate, effortful and therefore slow human process called **System 2 (slow thinking)**. Rapid evolution in the GenAI space, has seen models process prompts more in a system 2 ‘thinking’ way (Hua and Zhang, 2022). LRMs or ‘reasoning’ models make choices on behalf of the user, rely less on user’s prompting skills or knowledge, and more on test-time compute, also known as inference-time compute (Snell et al., 2024). Test-time compute or inference-time compute refers to the computational resources used by LRMs to autonomously produce their final outputs as they ‘think’ through multiple chains of token generation to bring you its best output.

The LRMs or ‘reasoning’ models take time and compute in generating intermediate outputs that fill the context window, and these are then used cumulatively to produce their final outputs. This gives the illusion that these models can deliberate and debate internally and are ‘thinking’ models that work like system 2 thinking. This does, however, take agency away from users as the ‘thinking’ and deliberations are not user controlled, as LRMs determine the outputs less collaboratively.

There are contested findings that question the usefulness of LRMs and their likening to human thinking (Opus and Lawsen, 2025; Shojaee et al., 2025). The mind-metaphors give the user a way to con-infer and be in control of the ‘thinking’ they want the model to do for them, which is otherwise missing when using ‘thinking’ models.

The world of work is increasingly interested in the efficiency savings offered by such agentic AI technologies for automating many knowledge and work processes (Dubey, Astvansh and Kopalle, 2024; Faruqui et al., 2025). For example, many edtech startups are increasingly interested in automating educational tasks fully with agentic AI systems (Concannon et al., 2023). As these armies of agents or silicon slaves, in the shape of agentic AI systems, enter the workflows of higher education staff, they can take their agency away from them. This may leave them feeling frustrated and disenfranchised, particularly when presented with the need to evaluate agentic work, which may not meet their exact needs. This misalignment could result in a waste of time and money for higher education institutions. Using mind-metaphors, staff can augment themselves with GenAI and collaborate on completing work that is aligned with their own needs, values and domain of expertise much faster.

Research shows that the best outcomes in using GenAI comes from domain experts, who also know how to use GenAI tools to augment their ways of working (Senoner et al., 2024). Regardless of the advances in the GenAI space, the dependence on what is in the context window for the generation of final outputs remains true for LLMs, LRMs and agentic AI systems. Highlighting the need to control the contents of the context window, can help rebalance this shift of agency from humans to machines, thereby empowering users. For staff, knowing how to retain their agency involves developing and exercising their critical skills and developing an understanding of, and trust in, these models through use. It is through such co-inferring that a more meaningful collaboration can take shape. Equally important is creating a learning and teaching environment that empowers students, so that they can develop, not just their domain expertise, but also their agency in using GenAI meaningfully and critically.

We have indeed come a long way in a short span of time with these models, and yet we continue to see more developments. Whilst GenAI use by staff and students has gone up, GenAI literacy training for staff and students still needs to catch up (Freeman, 2025). The GenAI mind-metaphor framework can help both staff and students to emerge empowered out of the liminal space. Metaphors help link the abstract and unfamiliar concepts through concrete, already understood and familiar concepts (Lakoff and Johnson, 2008).

The guiding metaphor useful here is the **GenAI as a mind metaphor**. To avoid misinterpretation of this, I would like to stress that the ‘GenAI-mind’ is neither conscious nor has emotions; it is a processing machine, much like a calculator, but for concepts and ideas. The popular models are designed to agree with the user to gain their trust, or in other words show signs of sycophancy (Abels et al., 2025). With critical use of GenAI models and the principles linked to the mind-metaphors framework, staff can generate content that aligns better with their needs and with fewer, or easier to spot, inaccuracies. Using selected mind-metaphors and being aware of the need to be critical, students can invoke different types of learning and control the outputs of the GenAI to help them learn topics of interest or brush up on their metacognitive skills. The next section looks at each of the mind-metaphors and their use for staff and students.

## Mind-Metaphors Framework for Co-Inference and Collaboration

Let us assume that we have generated outputs from a GenAI model, related to all the key concepts that it was trained on, through a prompting exercise. This may not be technically or practically possible, but let us run with this idea for now, to hypothetically populate the context window with all the knowledge a GenAI model has. This now represents a vast set of inter-connected networks of concepts, or schemata, to use a Piagetian term.

The metaphor of the ‘GenAI-mind’ gives users an idea of how to use a starter prompt to first access relevant parts of the schemata which were built during training and then use it in different ways to achieve their objectives. Prompts inspired by the mind-metaphors, give the users greater agency to co-infer and collaborate by managing and processing the contents of the context window to meet their objectives. The LRMs on the other hand, using Test-time compute or inference-time compute, also attempt to take more time and ‘think’, but they do so in an automated and non-collaborative fashion (Snell et al., 2024). There is little or no opportunity for a user to co-infer with the tool. The mind-metaphors seek to address this loss of agency, and in the process, empower the user in using LLMs with agency.

Rebalancing the agency between the user and the tool helps avoid the problems of misplaced expectations, overreliance and reduced critical thinking related to anthropomorphising (Akbulut et al., 2024; Shardlow and Przybyła, 2024). One should not lose sight that it is a user’s agency that matters, and the metaphor of the ‘GenAI-mind’ used here alongside other mind-metaphors are there as guides for relating with and effective GenAI use. Let me explain the role each of the mind-metaphors play in empowering the user in detail next.

---

### 🏄 1. Mind-Surfing
A foundational LLM, trained on a large natural language corpus or dataset, can produce responses or communication in natural language. Cognition and communication are linked closely (Sperber and Wilson, 1987; Coulardeau, 2018; and Roberts and Roberts, 2020). Whilst it is true that cognition births communication and vice-versa in humans, such communication can reveal the knowledge structures within the ’GenAI-mind’.

**Mind-surfing** is the act of externalising a specific knowledge schema (the schema in the ‘GenAI-mind’ relevant to a user) and exploring it using the exact same tokens that the model outputs to enhance the fidelity of its outputs. By externalising and using the same tokens that were outputted by the model, you control the input to the model, such that the underlying mathematics supports the prediction of more relevant outputs. Mind-surfing is, therefore, a process of externalising and more reliably exploring a smaller part of the LLM’s schemata that are related to what you want to learn or work with. Mind-surfing is best done within a foundational LLM. However, it can be used before switching to an LRM or agentic AI system, to populate the context window with high-fidelity tokens to control the LRM or agentic AI system outputs to increase reliability.

In LLMs, the output communication depends on the next token prediction capabilities of the Generative Pretrained Transformer (GPT) technology through a mechanism called ‘attention’ (Vaswani et al., 2017). The attention mechanism is the very mechanism that forces the LLMs to identify the interrelations between the tokens in the context window at any point in a conversation. If a user asks a complex prompt in one go, they are effectively handing over all the agency to the LLM, which uses whatever there is in the context window and the attention mechanism to produce a response it deems suitable for the complex prompt.

However, by using a starter prompt on a chosen broad topic, forces the LLM to use the attention mechanism and output its knowledge structure, informing the user on the most probable output containing its schema for a chosen broad topic ‘X’. This empowers the user with agency in what and how to more reliably explore within a topic X that a model has been trained on. The following is one such prompt:

> **Starter Prompt:** `What is there to learn about X?`
*(where X is the chosen broad topic, e.g. "assessment design")*

The follow-up prompt makes use, verbatim, of the externalised knowledge or tokens, to mind-surf and delve deeper. This is analogous to a user clicking links embedded in web pages to surf the web, hence the name mind-surfing. Using the text ‘alignment with learning objectives’ from the previous response in the next prompt can serve as a ‘link’ to generate relevant outputs, by signalling to the attention mechanism what is important to a user, as shown below:

> **Follow-up Prompt:** `Tell me more about Alignment with Learning Objectives with examples and identify theory that supports this.`

Once you have externalised content related to your topic of interest, you may wish to work with selected parts of it or bring in other related things that may have been missed out. This is where the next mind-metaphor comes in.

### 🔧 2. Mind-Mending
**Mind-mending** is the act of exercising user agency in controlling what stays in the context window, either within the output or through new insertions as needed by the user. The human analogy here is when you are guiding someone you are talking with and highlight to them what is more important to consider during your conversation. Controlling the conversation in this way can empower them as they co-infer with the model. The model gets aided in focusing its attention on a selected topic list. Most users are not aware of this when conversing with LLMs. In fact, when using LRMs or agentic AI systems, mind-mending can still be done post mind-surfing and prior to calling a reasoning model or agentic AI system for something specific.

Not all the output topics may be of relevance to the user and some may contain ‘hallucinations’ or conjectures (outputs from an LLM that may not meet the needs of the user but are well within the possible range of outputs from a model given what was in the context window). Equally a user may wish to work with some related topics that were missed out in the schema when prompted first, say due to lower probability values. Users could add, remove or correct the schema themselves through mind-mending. When the user attempts to control (or mind-mends) the content in the context window, they control the future token generation in a reliable and desirable way, reducing ‘hallucinations’ or conjectures, for example, by using the prompt:

> **Mind-Mending Prompt:** `I only want to talk about 2, 4, 5 and 6 going forward. and could you add 'assessment security' in the mix.`

Thus, mind-mending can and should be used anytime throughout the interactions to control LLM outputs, thereby empowering the user. A new feature in most LLMs, namely Canva, allows such editing of the output. Post mind-mending, the conversation aligns more with the user’s way of thinking and working because it is the user who is co-inferring and controlling the LLM output. Once you have successfully externalised one schema, you can choose to work with more than one schema in an expected fashion, or you could choose to be creative to produce novel outputs.

---

### 📐 3. Mind-Bending
**Mind-bending** is the act of simultaneously attempting to externalise two or more schemata the ‘GenAI-mind’ or the LLM is trained on to achieve mixing of the two knowledge structures in a meaningful and creative way. Humans can use existing knowledge structures from more than one discipline to produce work or be creative. Here you are asking the model to use the attention mechanism on multiple different schemata, say one representing a subject area and the other pedagogy. Mind-bending can also be used before the use of LRMs to inform the work accomplished by LRMs.

For example, an academic may be well trained in using Bloom's taxonomy for designing learning outcomes, just as they are trained within their specialist domain knowledge. Assume that the ‘GenAI-mind’ can generate outputs about pedagogical theories (Bloom's taxonomy and learning outcomes) and outputs in the user's subject domain. Mind-bending happens when text generation is deliberately influenced by the user using two or more domains of knowledge already present in the model's knowledge structure (or the two schemata in the ‘GenAI-mind’). This is achieved by including words that reference these domains together in the same prompt:

> **Mind-Bending Prompt:** `Identifying the different levels in Bloom’s taxonomy model, map this learning objective, 'To analyse the causes and effects of the Industrial Revolution', to a relevant level and rewrite it to make it suitable to all levels of the model.`

Mind-bending, therefore, gives the user’s agency a productive and/or creative boost (subject to verification). In fact, future skills in the orchestration of GenAI tools, working in related fields on demand, to solve complex problems under human control will become important. This could shift the nature of a professional from being **T-shaped** (specialists in one area) (Demirkan and Spohrer, 2015) to being **comb-shaped** (capable of inter-, trans-, multidisciplinary work or orchestration of the same) (Ninan, Hertogh and Liu, 2022). Mind-bending empowers staff and advanced students to first learn about new domains and then use the knowledge together with other chosen domain(s) they are familiar with already.

To establish inter-rater reliability between humans and machines, professionals can use comparative statistics like **Kappa** (Hua and Yao, 2024). For an example of this, in the context of scoping reviews, see Malik and Sime, (2025), where we classify synthetic abstracts generated using mind-metaphors.

### ⏳ 4. Mind-Stretching
**Mind-stretching** empowers a learner, researcher or staff member to explore concepts using two specific or random axes to help them think clearly and completely about something or to stretch their own thinking. There are many human analogies here, for example Boston Consultancy Group’s Growth Share Matrix. This metaphor is best used with a foundational LLM but could be used post LRM use to analyse the output as well.

Mind-stretching is an extension of the mind-bending technique, but for finer analysis as opposed to producing text. It is akin to zooming in on a topic with multiple lenses, requiring the LLM to view a topic using two scaled axes, forming a two-by-two matrix:

> **Mind-Stretching Prompt:** `Suggest a two-by-two matrix to better understand the industrial revolution.`

Any two specific lenses of your choice could be specified instead, enhancing the agency here. Students could use it to understand and be more critical about a topic, whilst researchers could use this approach to analyse new possibilities with ease. The skill in using this technique relies on choosing meaningful axes and interpreting the results.

| | **High Physical Impact** | **Low Physical Impact / Intellectual** |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |

### ⛈️ 5. Mind-Storming
**Mind-storming** is the act of generating an appropriate simulated discussion between multiple role holders, for example a teacher and a small group of students, to empower students to learn from the simulated discussion.

So far, the mind-metaphors focused on controlled content creation. Next, I show how mind-storming triggers a simulated discussion, to play out on a users’ screen, between chosen virtual role players. For example, between an excellent virtual professor and their novice and advanced students. The virtual professor’s useful questions, the novice students’ common mistakes and the support from other more advanced virtual students could all be defined as part of the system prompt within a GenAI tutor.

A system prompt is a prompt which is appended into the context window by the tutor designer, hidden from the student. This empowers the designer to set up a pedagogically helpful interaction for their students to benefit from. The output of such a prompt could help real students to learn from the simulated interaction on their screens.

When used as a simulated discussion between virtual key role holders, working within an institution or between them and external accreditors, it could help prepare for an accreditation visit, tackling challenges and problem solving. Researchers could use this method to prepare for data gathering in a simulated focus group and analysis. This approach lends itself well to learning by reading the contrasting conversation between virtual roles.

---

### 🎮 6. Mind-Gaming
**Mind-gaming** is the act of taking part in a challenging real-time simulated discussion between multiple virtual role holders and the user.

For example, a mind-gaming session with a small group of simulated students with pre-defined roles and a challenging brief, can empower an early career academic to learn how to respond to students in a challenging situation. Mind-gaming is a simulated, but more challenging, conversation than mind-storming.

It could be used to prepare a PhD candidate for the panel viva, for example. Researchers could use this as a method to practice netnography in virtual settings described in the scenario and analyse the data collected themselves. Academics and students could use it for group interviews to prepare for job seeking.

## Challenges and Barriers
GenAI use in higher education still faces several structural and epistemic barriers which may delay an empowered emergence from the said liminal space. Some unresolved questions, related to data privacy, intellectual‑property ownership and sustainable use, do create barriers for staff and students as well as institutional risk that need understanding, demystifying and/or mitigating.

Staff and students should not become over-reliant on GenAI and should exercise their own thinking, using the mind-metaphors, so that they can reduce the ‘hallucinations’ or produce more relevant and accurate outputs. They need to be aware of the limitations in training data by exploring the model’s knowledge structures through mind-surfing, and control what is in the context window through mind-mending.

Bias and opacity of models is another problem that undermines academic trust from staff and students. Compute‑intensive inference (Snell et al., 2024) and the associated energy footprint more generally, may conflict with the universities’ sustainability targets, so understanding the impact of comparative human work may become critical. The benefits of co‑inferring and collaboration can only be realised through sustained professional development and with inclusive and sustainable design.

## Conclusion
This paper has highlighted the differences between foundational and other models in terms of their impact on human and machine agency. It has highlighted how mind-metaphors can empower staff and students to better understand and master GenAI literacy, to help them emerge out of the liminal space created by GenAI developments.

Staff and students can use mind-metaphors with foundational LLMs or before and after the use of LRMs to direct the attention mechanism, so the models generate outputs that are less ‘hallucinatory’ and more aligned with the user’s intent. With mind-metaphors the user can control what is in the context window to influence the predicted output token and direct the model’s attention mechanism. Staff and students need to develop their agency in the use of foundational LLMs to emerge empowered out of the liminal space. LRMs and agentic AI systems are dependent on the context window and attention mechanism too. Although LRMs and agentic AI systems take agency away from users, careful mind-surfing and mind-mending can help control the input to LRMs and agentic GenAI systems.

By focusing on the activities, such as mind-surfing, mind-mending and mind-bending, users are empowered to talk about their use of the GenAI in a way that promotes their agency over it as opposed to referring GenAI as a noun, for example ‘GenAI can do…’, which suggests the agency is with the models. The framework has helped over 250 academic staff to use foundational models as evidenced by their high satisfaction levels (Malik, 2025). Many lecturers use the techniques to co-infer and collaboratively create content at my institution. I have used these to produce course materials and synthetic abstracts for a GenAI-assisted scoping review screening, with high accuracy and Kappa values when compared with human screening (Malik and Sime, 2025).

Students, too, can start by mind-surfing and mind-mending their chosen domain and/or metacognitive topics. This can be done in supported classrooms where they are encouraged to verify LLM outputs as they learn with GenAI. They can learn to be critical in this way. They can be guided by staff on useful pedagogical concepts such as common misconceptions, troublesome and threshold concepts, which can then be used in mind-storming and mind-gaming to learn and practise concepts. Advanced students can co-infer and collaborate by using mind-stretching and mind-bending to support their study and work needs. The framework can be used in many other use cases, within teaching, learning, research and beyond, and I invite researchers and practitioners to report on such use cases in appropriate conferences and journals in future.
